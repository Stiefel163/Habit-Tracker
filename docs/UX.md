# Comeback: UX decisions

## The latest lesson: lean beats complete

On October 19 he said, honestly: *"Right now I live with none of these habits. Exams next week. The other things are all extras that take far too much effort. You're connecting too many things at once."* Nine daily checks, four progress systems and a weekly form are a plan for a person who already has habits. For someone starting from zero, they're a wall.

So the app now works like this:

1. **Modes (seasons) set the focus.** Only 2–4 habits are visible on Today; everything else is paused, not failed. Exam mode is study + sleep + clean day. Build mode is morning lock-in + training + study. Off-grid is clean day + training.
2. **One daily question:** *Did I lock in?* (all focus done).
3. **One progress system:** the tree for today, Kanye eras as the level (one step per locked-in day) and the streak with freezes. Goals and animals are removed; he said they didn't move him.
4. **Habits come back one at a time** (*Atomic Habits* / *Tiny Habits*). After 10 of 14 locked-in days in Build mode, the app offers the next habit, easiest first.
5. **Accountability in one tap:** the Sunday check-in is one optional line. The urge button and the cookie jar stay, because those are what he responds to.
6. **Study blocks are the schedule.** In Exam mode, 4 slots of 90 min turn "study all day" into four concrete finish lines. The tree grows with each block.

## What we count, and why (research)

- **Measure only what tells you where you stand.** In a meta-analysis of 138 experiments, monitoring progress improved goal attainment (d ≈ 0.40), especially when progress was physically recorded (Harkin et al., 2016). So the two things with a real finish line get numbers:
  - **Bögen** (practice sheets), with passed / not passed. Passing means ≤ 10 error points in the class B exam, so "passed of your last 10" is a direct readiness signal.
  - **Study time**, as quick blocks. Blocks give minutes *and* sessions with one tap, and there's no timer to start.
- Everything else stays a single check, because a number wouldn't change what you do next.
- **Morning lock-in is an if-then plan.** "When I wake up → out of bed → breakfast → start something" is an implementation intention, which has a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d ≈ 0.65, Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). It stays separate from sleep: good sleep makes getting up easier, but the lock-in is about what you do in the first hour.

## Lock in: cutting cheap dopamine

The core problem is being hooked on short-form video, not a lack of knowledge. The design follows the four laws from *Atomic Habits*, applied in both directions:

| Law | Good habits | Reels (inverted) | In the app |
|---|---|---|---|
| Obvious | Minimum version under each habit, stacked on routines ("with breakfast") | Phone sleeps outside the bedroom | Phone setup checklist |
| Attractive | Identity: "I'm someone who locks in", fuel lines, Kanye eras | | Lock in header |
| Easy | 2-minute versions count fully | Friction: the one sec app (people cancelled 36% of openings and tried 37% less often over 6 weeks in a PNAS 2023 study), grayscale on triple-click | Setup |
| Satisfying | Tree, streak, eras | Each beaten urge counts and turns into a rep | Urge flow, cookie jar |

On top of that:
- **Duolingo:** a small daily goal and a streak with earned freezes. That is "never miss twice" built into the streak instead of punishment.
- **Goggins:** the 40% rule on the urge screen, the cookie jar, and the accountability mirror.
- **Dopamine Nation:** a 30-day reset from the one specific "drug".
- **The national-park insight:** when he was busy and offline he didn't miss the feed. So the urge flow offers concrete replacement actions, and the setup asks for one offline adventure a week.
- **Instagram stays for posting and DMs.** He is a creator, so the rule is "no feed", not "no Instagram". The laptop stays work-only.

## Removed on purpose

The pixel world, the cabin, "strong weeks", sleep clock times, training splits and durations, Spanish minutes, the week and month analytics, and the journal line. Each one was something you had to learn or fill in, and none of them helped you decide whether you'd done the thing.
